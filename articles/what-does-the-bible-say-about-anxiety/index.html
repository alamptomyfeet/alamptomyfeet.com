What Does the Bible Say About Anxiety?

An honest, hopeful look at what Scripture actually says to anxious hearts.

October 16, 2026 · 4 min read

“Do not be anxious about anything, but in everything by prayer and supplication with thanksgiving let your requests be made known to God. And the peace of God, which surpasses all understanding, will guard your hearts and your minds in Christ Jesus.”

Philippians 4:6–7 (ESV)

The Bible talks about anxiety more honestly than many of us expect. It never pretends life is free of reasons to worry, and it never treats anxious people as a problem to be fixed. Instead, it keeps making the same tender move: it takes the worry out of your own head and invites you to place it in God's hands.

The clearest summary is Philippians 4:6–7. Don't carry your anxieties alone. Bring every one of them to God in prayer, with thanksgiving, and His peace will stand guard over your heart and mind in Christ Jesus. Around that passage, Scripture adds Jesus' words about the birds and the lilies (Matthew 6:25–34), Peter's invitation to cast our anxieties on God because he cares for you (1 Peter 5:7), and dozens of psalms that pray straight through fear.

Scripture is honest about anxious hearts

Before the Bible ever tells us not to be anxious, it shows us real people who were. Martha bustled through her kitchen until Jesus said, Martha, Martha, you are anxious and troubled about many things (Luke 10:41). Notice that He says her name twice. That isn't a scolding. It is the way you speak to someone you love who has been running too hard.

The apostle Paul admits the daily pressure on me of my anxiety for all the churches (2 Corinthians 11:28). The psalmist writes, When the cares of my heart are many, your consolations cheer my soul (Psalm 94:19). And in Gethsemane, Jesus Himself said, My soul is very sorrowful, even to death (Matthew 26:38).

Whole psalms are given over to this kind of honesty. Psalm 42 lets a believer argue with her own downcast soul, and Psalm 56 holds fear and trust in a single breath: When I am afraid, I put my trust in you (Psalm 56:3). Lament is not the opposite of faith. In Scripture, it is often what faith sounds like on a hard day.

So if you have wondered whether your anxiety disqualifies you from God's nearness, the Bible's answer is clear. These pages were written for people exactly like you.

Prayer instead of carrying it alone

When Paul writes do not be anxious about anything, he is not handing out a rule to feel calmer. Read the sentence right before it: The Lord is at hand (Philippians 4:5). The reason you can let go of anxiety is that someone is near enough to receive it.

Anxiety and prayer are aimed at the very same unknowns. One turns them over endlessly in your own mind; the other carries them to God. That is why Paul can say in everything. Nothing is too small, too repetitive, or too embarrassing to bring. Supplication simply means asking, humbly and specifically. God is not bothered by your details. He invites them.

And he adds with thanksgiving. Thanksgiving isn't pretending things are fine. It is remembering who you are talking to: the God who has been faithful before and who gave His own Son for you. Gratitude steadies prayer, because it reminds an anxious heart of what is already true.

A peace that stands guard

Paul's word for guard was a military word, the picture of a garrison keeping watch over a city. His first readers lived in Philippi, a Roman colony (Acts 16:12) that knew the sight of soldiers. And Paul was writing in chains, with his imprisonment known throughout the whole imperial guard (Philippians 1:13). A man watched by soldiers tells his friends they are being watched over too, by a far better guard.

Notice what kind of peace it is: one which surpasses all understanding. You don't need to have everything figured out to be kept by it. The circumstances may stay unsettled for a while. God's peace takes its post anyway, at the door of the heart and mind, the very places anxiety tries to get in.

Tim Keller, in his 1990 sermon "Peace – Overcoming Anxiety" on Philippians 4:4–9, observed that many Christians lose heart simply because they never expected their peace to come under attack. That is a freeing thought. Feeling your peace contested doesn't mean you have lost it. (Paraphrased.)

Where our peace is anchored

It would be easy to turn Philippians 4 into a technique: pray this way, feel better. But the peace Paul describes rests on something deeper than a method. Since we have been justified by faith, we have peace with God through our Lord Jesus Christ (Romans 5:1). Because Jesus died and rose again for us, we are no longer outsiders hoping God will listen. We are children speaking to a Father.

That doesn't mean anxiety simply vanishes. For many women, it is a long companion. Scripture's promise is not that you'll never feel anxious again, but that you will never face it alone. I will never leave you nor forsake you (Hebrews 13:5). The One who says that is the same Lord who is at hand in Philippians 4:5, near in His presence and faithful to His word.

Bringing it home

If you want to start somewhere today, read Philippians 4:4–7 slowly, perhaps twice. Then write down the specific things you have been carrying and pray through them one by one, adding one thing you are thankful for. You don't have to feel peaceful first. You only need to bring what you have to the One who is near. And if anxiety has grown heavy, a pastor or Christian counselor can walk alongside you as you keep bringing it to Him.

Questions women often ask

Does the Bible say not to worry?

Yes. Jesus says do not be anxious in Matthew 6, and Paul says the same in Philippians 4. But both ground the command in God's care: the Father knows what you need, and the Lord is near. It is an invitation to trust, not a demand to stop feeling.

Is anxiety a sin according to the Bible?

Feeling anxious is not, in itself, a sin. Faithful people in Scripture felt deep distress. Worry becomes a spiritual problem when it hardens into settled distrust of God. We explore this more fully in Is Anxiety a Sin?

What is the most comforting Bible verse for anxiety?

Many people turn first to Philippians 4:6–7 and 1 Peter 5:7. Psalm 94:19 and Isaiah 41:10 are also deeply loved. The best verse is often the one you read slowly enough to pray.
