Matthew 5:28 Meaning: What Jesus Said About the Heart

What Jesus meant by looking with lustful intent, who He was speaking to, and the grace that surrounds His words.

November 14, 2026 · 5 min read

“But I say to you that everyone who looks at a woman with lustful intent has already committed adultery with her in his heart.”

Matthew 5:28 (ESV)

Matthew 5:28 is one of the most quoted and most misunderstood sentences Jesus ever said. Some read it and feel condemned for every glance. Others read it as an impossible standard and quietly give up. Read in its setting, it says something both more serious and more hopeful than either.

Matthew 5:28 means that lust is a matter of the heart, not only the body. Jesus teaches that a look aimed at wanting someone who is not yours is already sin, even if nothing is ever acted on. He is not condemning a passing thought or noticing that someone is attractive.

What does Matthew 5:28 say?

In the ESV, Jesus says, But I say to you that everyone who looks at a woman with lustful intent has already committed adultery with her in his heart (Matthew 5:28). The verse just before it sets it up: You have heard that it was said, 'You shall not commit adultery.' (Matthew 5:27).

Jesus is quoting the seventh commandment, given to Israel at Sinai, and then taking His listeners beneath it. The commandment named the act. Jesus names where the act begins.

Who was Jesus speaking to in Matthew 5:28?

These words come from the Sermon on the Mount (Matthew 5–7), spoken to His disciples with crowds listening in. Earlier in the same sermon He had said, For I tell you, unless your righteousness exceeds that of the scribes and Pharisees, you will never enter the kingdom of heaven (Matthew 5:20). Many in His day measured faithfulness by outward behavior. If you had never committed adultery, you had kept the commandment.

Jesus shows that God was always after more than behavior. The tenth commandment already forbade coveting a neighbor's wife (Exodus 20:17). In the next chapters He keeps pointing to the heart, and later He says, For out of the heart come evil thoughts, murder, adultery, sexual immorality, theft, false witness, slander (Matthew 15:19).

What does "lustful intent" mean?

The key phrase is lustful intent. Jesus is describing a look with a purpose: a gaze that lingers in order to want, imagine and possess someone who does not belong to you. That is why He connects it to adultery. It is the heart already taking what it has no right to.

It is not the same as noticing that someone is attractive, or a thought that arrives uninvited and is turned away. Jesus Himself was tempted, as Hebrews says: For we do not have a high priest who is unable to sympathize with our weaknesses, but one who in every respect has been tempted as we are, yet without sin (Hebrews 4:15). Temptation is not the sin. The welcomed, fed, chosen look is. We look at that line more closely in Is lust a sin?

Jesus spoke of a man looking at a woman because that was the commandment's setting and His audience, but the principle about the heart applies to everyone, women and men alike.

Does Jesus mean to tear out your eye?

The very next verse says, If your right eye causes you to sin, tear it out and throw it away. For it is better that you lose one of your members than that your whole body be thrown into hell (Matthew 5:29). Jesus is using deliberate overstatement, a common way of teaching in His culture, to show how seriously sin should be taken. He is not asking anyone to harm their body. An eye alone cannot make a heart pure, which is His whole point.

What He is calling for is decisive action: cutting off what feeds the sin rather than managing it. In practice that can mean changing habits, setting real boundaries, and inviting others in. There are more practical, Scripture-based steps.

Is there grace in Matthew 5:28?

Yes, and it is close by. The same sermon opens with blessing on the needy, not the impressive: Blessed are the poor in spirit, for theirs is the kingdom of heaven (Matthew 5:3), and, Blessed are the pure in heart, for they shall see God (Matthew 5:8). Jesus raises the standard so that no one can claim to meet it on their own, and then He offers Himself to those who know they can't.

The rest of the New Testament says it plainly: If we confess our sins, he is faithful and just to forgive us our sins and to cleanse us from all unrighteousness (1 John 1:9). And for those who belong to Him, There is therefore now no condemnation for those who are in Christ Jesus (Romans 8:1). The call to a pure heart comes with the promise of a cleansed one.

What does Matthew 5:28 mean for me today?

If you have carried guilt over every glance, let this verse lift a false burden: noticing is not lusting. If you have been feeding a look you know is wrong, Jesus is not trying to shame you. He is calling you to honesty, forgiveness and a new way of seeing people, as image-bearers rather than objects.

You don't have to fight alone. A trusted pastor, an accountability partner or a Christian counselor is a wise step, and if you ever feel unsafe or in crisis, please reach out to local help right away. For the wider picture, read What does the Bible say about lust?

Questions women often ask

What is the meaning of Matthew 5:28?

Jesus teaches that lust begins in the heart. Looking at someone "with lustful intent" (Matthew 5:28), wanting someone who is not yours, is already adultery in the heart, even if it is never acted on.

Is it a sin to look at someone and find them attractive?

No. Noticing that someone is attractive, or being tempted, is not what Jesus condemns. He was "tempted as we are, yet without sin" (Hebrews 4:15). Matthew 5:28 is about a deliberate look meant to desire what is not yours.

Does Matthew 5:28 apply to women?

Yes. Jesus spoke of a man looking at a woman because that was the commandment's setting and His audience, but the principle about the heart applies to everyone, women and men alike. Looking "with lustful intent" (Matthew 5:28) is already sin in the heart, whether the one looking is a woman or a man.

Does Matthew 5:29 mean I should literally tear out my eye?

No. Jesus uses deliberate overstatement to show how seriously to take sin. He calls for decisive action against what feeds sin, not harm to the body, since sin starts in the heart.

Can God forgive lust?

Yes. "If we confess our sins, he is faithful and just to forgive us our sins and to cleanse us from all unrighteousness" (1 John 1:9).
