Is It Okay to Be Angry at God?

What the Psalms show us about bringing our hardest feelings to Him.

November 3, 2026 · 4 min read

“Trust in him at all times, O people; pour out your heart before him; God is a refuge for us.”

Psalm 62:8 (ESV)

Maybe it hit at the graveside, or months later in the car. Somewhere in your grief a feeling rose up that scared you: you are angry at God. He could have stopped this, and He didn't.

Many believers hide that feeling because they think it means their faith is failing. Scripture is more honest than that. It gives us whole prayers full of raw complaint, aimed straight at God, and it also shows the difference between anger that runs to Him and anger that turns away from Him.

The Bible is full of honest complaint

David prayed, How long, O LORD? Will you forget me forever? How long will you hide your face from me? (Psalm 13:1). That is not a polite prayer.

Psalm 88, a psalm of Heman the Ezrahite, goes further. O LORD, why do you cast my soul away? Why do you hide your face from me? (Psalm 88:14). It is one of the darkest prayers in the Bible, and it never turns to praise. It ends, my companions have become darkness (Psalm 88:18). God still put that prayer in His Word.

The prophet Habakkuk watched violence and injustice spread through his own nation and asked, O LORD, how long shall I cry for help, and you will not hear? (Habakkuk 1:2). God did not strike him down for asking. He answered him.

Jesus prayed a lament

On the cross, about the ninth hour Jesus cried out with a loud voice, saying, "Eli, Eli, lema sabachthani?" that is, "My God, my God, why have you forsaken me?" (Matthew 27:46). He was quoting the first line of Psalm 22 (Psalm 22:1). The sinless Son of God brought His anguish to His Father in the words of a lament. Asking God "why" is not, by itself, rebellion.

Notice who they are talking to

Here is what these prayers have in common. Lament is anger that keeps talking to God. David, Heman and Habakkuk kept praying, bringing everything to the only One who could help. Psalm 62:8 invites exactly this: pour out your heart before him; God is a refuge for us.

Job is a sober example. After losing his children and everything he owned, he said, The LORD gave, and the LORD has taken away; blessed be the name of the LORD (Job 1:21). Later his grief poured out in long, bitter complaints. When God answered out of the whirlwind, He corrected Job: Who is this that darkens counsel by words without knowledge? (Job 38:1–2). Job saw God and repented (Job 42:6). Yet God then told Job's friends, you have not spoken of me what is right, as my servant Job has (Job 42:7).

When anger becomes bitterness

There is another kind of anger in Scripture. When God showed mercy to Nineveh, Jonah was furious that He had relented. God asked him, Do you do well to be angry? (Jonah 4:4). Jonah's anger was not a cry for help but a demand that God be less merciful.

Asaph knew how close the line can be. Watching the wicked prosper, he wrote, When my soul was embittered, when I was pricked in heart, I was brutish and ignorant; I was like a beast toward you (Psalm 73:21–22). Then comes the turn: Nevertheless, I am continually with you; you hold my right hand (Psalm 73:23). Even when his heart had gone sour, God had not let go of him.

So the question is not only "Am I angry?" but "Where is my anger taking me?" Toward God with honest questions, or away from Him with a verdict that He is not good?

Where lament leads

Most laments end somewhere new. Psalm 13, which began with How long, closes with But I have trusted in your steadfast love; my heart shall rejoice in your salvation (Psalm 13:5). Habakkuk's book, which began with complaint, ends with one of the most defiant songs of faith in the Bible: Though the fig tree should not blossom, nor fruit be on the vines... yet I will rejoice in the LORD; I will take joy in the God of my salvation (Habakkuk 3:17–18).

That turn rarely happens in a day, but you are not waiting alone. The LORD is near to the brokenhearted and saves the crushed in spirit (Psalm 34:18). There is more on that promise in Psalm 34:18 meaning.

For today

Pray Psalm 13 out loud as your own prayer, and tell God plainly what you are angry about. If heaven has felt quiet, What to do when God is silent may help. If your grief is for your mom or for a baby you lost, see Bible verses for losing a mother or Bible verses for miscarriage.

If the anger or grief feels too heavy to carry alone, a pastor or Christian counselor can help you carry it.

Questions women often ask

Is it a sin to be angry at God?

Feeling angry in grief is not the same as rebelling against God. David asked, "How long, O LORD? Will you forget me forever?" (Psalm 13:1), and God kept that prayer in Scripture. What matters is whether your anger keeps bringing you to Him or hardens into a verdict against Him.

Does "be angry and do not sin" apply to anger at God?

Ephesians 4:26 is about anger between people. Paul is writing about how believers treat one another (Ephesians 4:25–32), so it is not a verse about being angry at God. Its warning still helps, though: anger left to fester turns into the bitterness Paul says to put away (Ephesians 4:31).

What should I pray when I'm angry at God?

Start with a lament psalm like Psalm 13 or Psalm 88 and pray it as your own. Psalm 62:8 invites you to "pour out your heart before him; God is a refuge for us."
