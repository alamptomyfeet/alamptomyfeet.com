Seek First the Kingdom: What Matthew 6:33 Means for Worry

Jesus' answer to the anxious heart, read where He spoke it.

October 27, 2026 · 5 min read

“But seek first the kingdom of God and his righteousness, and all these things will be added to you.”

Matthew 6:33 (ESV)

Matthew 6:33 is one of the most quoted verses in the Bible. It shows up on mugs, in graduation cards and on the first page of goal planners. Sometimes it is quoted as if it were a deal: put God first, and He will give you the things you want.

Read where Jesus said it, though, and the verse sounds different. It is the closing word of a short sermon on worry, and "all these things" are not wishes. They are food, drink and clothing. It is a promise for the anxious, not a formula for the ambitious.

Where the verse sits

In the Sermon on the Mount, just after saying no one can serve two masters (Matthew 6:24), Jesus turns to the people listening and says, Therefore I tell you, do not be anxious about your life, what you will eat or what you will drink, nor about your body, what you will put on (Matthew 6:25).

That is the subject of the whole passage, verses 25 through 34. Not success, not a dream job, but the plain daily needs that keep people awake at night. Jesus asks, Is not life more than food, and the body more than clothing?

Look at the birds, consider the lilies

Then He points outside. Look at the birds of the air: they neither sow nor reap nor gather into barns, and yet your heavenly Father feeds them. Are you not of more value than they? (Matthew 6:26).

And He asks a question every worrier knows the answer to: And which of you by being anxious can add a single hour to his span of life? (v. 27). Worry feels like work. It feels responsible. But it does not add a single hour.

He goes on to the flowers: Consider the lilies of the field, how they grow: they neither toil nor spin (v. 28), and yet God dresses them more beautifully than Solomon. But if God so clothes the grass of the field, which today is alive and tomorrow is thrown into the oven, will he not much more clothe you, O you of little faith? (v. 30).

"Little faith" here is not a scolding. It is a Father gently pointing out that His children have forgotten who is caring for them.

Your Father knows

Here is the hinge of the passage: Therefore do not be anxious, saying, 'What shall we eat?' or 'What shall we drink?' or 'What shall we wear?' For the Gentiles seek after all these things, and your heavenly Father knows that you need them all (Matthew 6:31–32).

Jesus does not say the needs are unimportant. He says they are known. The Father who sees the sparrow and the field already has your grocery list, your rent and your child's winter coat in view.

So what does "seek first" mean?

Only then comes verse 33: But seek first the kingdom of God and his righteousness, and all these things will be added to you.

To seek His kingdom is to want God's rule in your life and your world more than anything else, and to seek His righteousness is to want to live the way He calls you to live. It is a change in what you are reaching for. Instead of spending your strength chasing security, you rest your security in Him and spend your strength on Him.

"All these things" points straight back to verses 31 and 32: what to eat, what to drink, what to wear. Luke records Jesus saying the same thing to His disciples, and adds a tender line right after: Fear not, little flock, for it is your Father's good pleasure to give you the kingdom (Luke 12:32). The kingdom is not a prize you earn by seeking well. It is a gift your Father is glad to give.

So Matthew 6:33 is not a promise that putting God first will bring wealth, a spouse or a certain career. It is a promise that the people who belong to Him will not be forgotten in their daily needs, and an invitation to stop carrying tomorrow on today's shoulders.

One day at a time

Jesus ends the passage with a sentence that sounds almost like advice from a grandmother: Therefore do not be anxious about tomorrow, for tomorrow will be anxious for itself. Sufficient for the day is its own trouble (Matthew 6:34).

He does not pretend there will be no trouble. He simply gives you permission to carry only today's.

A few ways to practice it

Name the worry out loud. Write down what you are anxious about, then read Matthew 6:32 over it: your heavenly Father knows that you need them all.

Turn the worry into a request. Peter, likely echoing Psalm 55:22, tells us to cast our anxieties on God because he cares for you (1 Peter 5:7). There is more on that verse in Cast All Your Anxiety on Him.

Ask a better first question. Before "How will I make this work?" try "Lord, what does it look like to seek You today?"

Leave tomorrow for tomorrow. When your mind runs ahead at night, come back to verse 34. If your thoughts tend to spin, Bible verses for anxiety and overthinking may help, and for a wider view, What does the Bible say about anxiety?

If worry has become constant or is affecting your sleep, work or health, it is wise to talk with a pastor, a doctor or a Christian counselor. If you are in crisis, please call or text 988 (in the US) today.

Questions women often ask

What does Matthew 6:33 mean?

In context it is Jesus' answer to worry. After telling His listeners not to be anxious about food, drink and clothing, He says, "But seek first the kingdom of God and his righteousness, and all these things will be added to you." Make God's rule and His ways your first concern, and trust your Father with your daily needs.

What are "all these things" in Matthew 6:33?

They are the things named in the verses just before it: what you will eat, drink and wear (Matthew 6:31-32). Jesus says, "your heavenly Father knows that you need them all."

Is Matthew 6:33 a promise of wealth or success?

No. The passage is about ordinary daily needs, not getting what we want. It promises that God cares for His children, and invites them to stop carrying tomorrow's worries today.
