Bible Verses for Grief After Miscarriage

For the mother whose arms are empty, and whose grief is real.

October 17, 2026 · 4 min read

“The LORD is near to the brokenhearted and saves the crushed in spirit.”

Psalm 34:18 (ESV)

If you have lost a baby through miscarriage, we are so sorry. Whether you were a few weeks along or many months, whether anyone else knew or not, your grief is real. You lost a child you were already loving, and a future you had already begun to imagine.

Scripture doesn't rush a grieving mother. Psalm 34:18 is often the first place to rest: The LORD is near to the brokenhearted and saves the crushed in spirit. Alongside it, Psalm 139:13–16, Isaiah 40:11, Psalm 56:8, John 11:35, 2 Samuel 12:23, and Revelation 21:4 have carried women through the hardest days. They don't explain the loss away. They show you who God is in the middle of it.

Your baby was known

In Psalm 139, David marvels at how God formed him: you knitted me together in my mother's womb (Psalm 139:13). A few verses later he writes, Your eyes saw my unformed substance (Psalm 139:16).

The Hebrew word behind unformed substance is golem, and it appears only here in the whole Old Testament. It describes life in its earliest, not-yet-finished shape, the kind of life no one else could see. But God saw.

That means your baby was never a "nothing" to Him. Before anyone else knew, before you could feel a kick, before there was a name or a nursery, your child was seen, known, and held in God's attention. The same God who knit your little one together was present for every day of that life, however short. It mattered to the One who made it.

The Lord who comes near

Look closely at Psalm 34:18. It doesn't say the LORD is near to the strong, or to those who have stopped crying. He is near to the brokenhearted. Grief does not push Him away. It is exactly where He promises to be.

The psalm's writer knew this from experience: I sought the LORD, and he answered me and delivered me from all my fears (Psalm 34:4). And he knew God keeps account of sorrow: You have kept count of my tossings; put my tears in your bottle. Are they not in your book? (Psalm 56:8). Not one sleepless night, not one tear, has gone unnoticed by Him.

When Jesus stood at the grave of His friend Lazarus, knowing He was about to raise him, the Bible says simply, Jesus wept (John 11:35). Isaiah had described Him centuries before as a man of sorrows and acquainted with grief (Isaiah 53:3). He did not scold the grieving. He grieved with them. Your tears are not a lack of faith. You do not have to be strong for God. He is strong for you.

A Shepherd for grieving mothers

Isaiah gives one of the tenderest pictures of God in all of Scripture: He will tend his flock like a shepherd; he will gather the lambs in his arms; he will carry them in his bosom, and gently lead those that are with young (Isaiah 40:11). Those last words describe the mother sheep, the ones caring for their lambs. The Shepherd slows His pace for them.

You are still a mother, and that gentleness is for you. If you have been replaying every detail, searching for what you did wrong, hear this: most often there was nothing you could have done. When Jesus' disciples assumed someone must be to blame for a man's suffering, He refused that whole line of thinking (John 9:2–3). As a father shows compassion to his children, so the LORD shows compassion to those who fear him. For he knows our frame (Psalm 103:13–14). He is not standing over you with an accusation. He is carrying you.

The hope we hold

After King David's infant son died, he said, I shall go to him, but he will not return to me (2 Samuel 12:23). Many Christians across the centuries have found deep comfort in those words, and many theologians see in them a reason for hope that little ones who die are safe with the Lord. Scripture doesn't answer every question we might ask, so we hold this humbly. But we hold it while trusting the character of a God who is rich in mercy (Ephesians 2:4).

And we hold it alongside the great promise at the end of the Bible: He will wipe away every tear from their eyes, and death shall be no more (Revelation 21:4). Jesus died and rose again so that death would not have the last word, not for us and not for those we have lost.

One small step

There is no right way to grieve a miscarriage, and no timeline you have to meet. Some mothers find it helps to give their baby a name, write a letter, or light a candle on the day they lost them. Others simply need to pray Psalm 34:18 again and again: The LORD is near. You might read Psalm 139:13–16 slowly and thank God that He saw your child when no one else could.

If you can, let one trusted friend or someone in your church know what happened. And if your husband is grieving differently than you, give each other grace, and perhaps read Psalm 34 aloud together. If the sorrow ever grows heavier than you can bear, please reach out to your pastor or a Christian counselor, or call or text 988 in a crisis.

Questions women often ask

Does God care about my miscarriage?

Yes. Psalm 139:16 shows that God sees life even in its earliest, unformed stage, and Psalm 34:18 promises that He is near to the brokenhearted. Your loss and your grief matter to Him.

Will I see my baby in heaven?

Scripture doesn't answer every detail, but many Christians find real hope in David's words about his infant son, I shall go to him (2 Samuel 12:23), and in the mercy of God shown in Christ. We hold that hope humbly and trust His goodness.

How long will this grief last?

There is no set timeline. Grief after miscarriage can return on due dates and anniversaries, sometimes for years. Jesus said, Blessed are those who mourn, for they shall be comforted (Matthew 5:4). Be patient with yourself, and lean on others.
