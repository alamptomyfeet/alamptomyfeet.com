Bible Verses for Grief After Miscarriage

For the mother whose arms are empty, and whose grief is real.

October 17, 2026 · 4 min read

“The LORD is near to the brokenhearted and saves the crushed in spirit.”

Psalm 34:18 (ESV)

If you have lost a baby through miscarriage, we are so sorry. Whether you were a few weeks along or many months, whether anyone else knew or not, your grief is real. You lost a child you were already loving, and a future you had already begun to imagine.

Many grieving mothers have found that Scripture doesn't rush them. Psalm 34:18 is often the first place to rest: The LORD is near to the brokenhearted and saves the crushed in spirit. Alongside it, Psalm 139:13–16, Psalm 56:8, John 11:35, 2 Samuel 12:23, and Revelation 21:4 have carried women through the hardest days. They don't explain the loss away. They sit with you in it.

Your baby was known

In Psalm 139, David marvels at how God formed him: you knitted me together in my mother's womb (Psalm 139:13). A few verses later he writes, Your eyes saw my unformed substance (Psalm 139:16).

The Hebrew word behind unformed substance is golem, and it appears only here in the whole Old Testament. It describes life in its earliest, not-yet-finished shape, the kind of life no one else could see. But God saw.

That means your baby was never a "nothing" to Him. Before anyone else knew, before you could feel a kick, before there was a name or a nursery, your child was seen, known, and held in God's attention. However short that little life was, it mattered to the One who made it.

Grief that God does not hurry

Miscarriage grief is often lonely. Sometimes very few people knew. Sometimes people say careless things, or nothing at all, and the world seems to move on before you have even begun to understand what happened.

Scripture moves differently. You have kept count of my tossings; put my tears in your bottle. Are they not in your book? (Psalm 56:8). Not one sleepless night, not one tear, has gone unnoticed by God.

And when Jesus stood at the grave of His friend Lazarus, knowing He was about to raise him, the Bible says simply, Jesus wept (John 11:35). He did not scold the grieving. He grieved with them. Your tears are not a lack of faith. They are love with nowhere else to go, and the Lord meets you in them. You do not have to be strong for God. He is strong for you.

When guilt creeps in

Many women replay everything: what they ate, how they slept, whether they lifted something or worried too much. It's worth hearing clearly that doctors say most miscarriages are not caused by anything a mother did. If you are carrying guilt that doesn't belong to you, you can bring it into the light and lay it down. God is not standing over you with an accusation. He is near to the brokenhearted.

Your body is grieving too. Recovery, hormones, and exhaustion are real, and sorrow after a loss can sometimes deepen into depression. Please talk with your doctor about how you are doing, physically and emotionally, and consider a counselor or a pastor who can walk with you. If you ever feel you can't go on, call or text 988 (the Suicide & Crisis Lifeline in the U.S.) right away. Asking for help is not weakness.

The hope we hold

After King David's infant son died, he said, I shall go to him, but he will not return to me (2 Samuel 12:23). Many Christians across the centuries have found deep comfort in those words, and many theologians see in them a reason for hope that little ones who die are safe with the Lord. Scripture doesn't answer every question we might ask, so we hold this humbly. But we hold it while trusting the character of a God who is rich in mercy (Ephesians 2:4).

And we hold it alongside the great promise at the end of the Bible: He will wipe away every tear from their eyes, and death shall be no more (Revelation 21:4). Jesus died and rose again so that death would not have the last word, not for us and not for those we have lost.

One small step

There is no right way to grieve a miscarriage, and no timeline you have to meet. Some mothers find it helps to give their baby a name, write a letter, plant something, or light a candle on the day they lost them. Others simply need to pray Psalm 34:18 again and again: The LORD is near.

If you can, let one trusted friend or someone in your church know what happened. And if your husband is grieving differently than you, give each other grace. You are both mourning the same child. Some couples find it helps to pray together, even briefly, or simply to read Psalm 34 aloud side by side. In time, God's nearness may begin to feel less like a doctrine and more like a presence in the room.

Questions women often ask

Does God care about my miscarriage?

Yes. Psalm 139:16 shows that God sees life even in its earliest, unformed stage, and Psalm 34:18 promises that He is near to the brokenhearted. Your loss and your grief matter to Him.

Will I see my baby in heaven?

Scripture doesn't answer every detail, but many Christians find real hope in David's words about his infant son, I shall go to him (2 Samuel 12:23), and in the mercy of God shown in Christ. We hold that hope humbly and trust His goodness.

How long will this grief last?

There is no set timeline. Grief after miscarriage can return on due dates and anniversaries, sometimes for years. Jesus said, Blessed are those who mourn, for they shall be comforted (Matthew 5:4). Be patient with yourself, and lean on others.
