Jeremiah 29:11 in Context

What “I know the plans I have for you” meant to exiles in Babylon, and what it means for you.

October 17, 2026 · 4 min read

“For I know the plans I have for you, declares the LORD, plans for welfare and not for evil, to give you a future and a hope.”

Jeremiah 29:11 (ESV)

Jeremiah 29:11 may be one of the most loved verses in the Bible. It shows up on graduation cards, wall art, and hospital bedside tables. And it deserves to be loved. But it means even more when we read it where God first spoke it.

Here is the short answer. Jeremiah 29:11 was part of a letter to Israelites living in exile in Babylon. God promised that after seventy years He would bring His people home, and that His purposes for them were good, even in a long and painful wait. It is not a guarantee of a particular career, relationship, or outcome. But it does reveal the heart of a God whose plans for His people hold, and for Christians, every one of His promises finds its Yes in Jesus (2 Corinthians 1:20).

A letter to people far from home

The chapter opens by telling us exactly what we are reading: the words of the letter that Jeremiah the prophet sent from Jerusalem to the exiles Nebuchadnezzar had carried off to Babylon (Jeremiah 29:1). These people had lost their homes, their temple, and their sense of where God was in all of it.

What God tells them first is surprising: Build houses and live in them; plant gardens and eat their produce (Jeremiah 29:5). In other words, unpack. Settle in. This is going to be long. A few verses later He names the timeline: When seventy years are completed for Babylon, I will visit you (Jeremiah 29:10). Many of the adults who first heard this letter would not live to see the return.

That makes the promise even more striking. Just one chapter earlier, a prophet named Hananiah had announced a much more comfortable message: everything would be restored within two years (Jeremiah 28:3). It was what everyone wanted to hear, and it wasn't true. Jeremiah 29:11 is not the easy promise. It is the true one, and the hope in it is sturdier for that.

The word behind "welfare"

The Hebrew word translated welfare in verse 11 is shalom, a word for peace, wholeness, and well-being. It appears just a few verses earlier, three times in one breath: seek the welfare of the city where I have sent you into exile, and pray to the LORD on its behalf, for in its welfare you will find your welfare (Jeremiah 29:7).

So God's plans for their shalom didn't begin with an escape route. They began right there in Babylon, as they built, planted, prayed, and waited. The good future was coming, but God's goodness was not on pause until then.

Some translations render verse 11 with the word "prosper," and it's easy to hear that as a promise of financial success or an easy life. The context points somewhere deeper: wholeness and restoration under God's care, worked out over time, often through hardship rather than around it.

Is Jeremiah 29:11 for me?

It's an honest question. The "you" in this verse is plural in Hebrew, spoken to the exiles as a people. So we shouldn't read it as God promising each of us a specific dream.

But we also don't need to set it aside. The verse shows us what God is like: a God who knows His plans, who intends good and not evil for His people, and who keeps His word across generations. He kept this promise, and He kept the far greater promise behind it in sending Jesus. That is why Paul can say that all the promises of God find their Yes in him (2 Corinthians 1:20). In Christ, you are part of the people God is bringing home.

Seeking Him in the waiting

Most people stop reading at verse 11, but the next two verses may be the heart of it: Then you will call upon me and come and pray to me, and I will hear you. You will seek me and find me, when you seek me with all your heart (Jeremiah 29:12–13).

The future and the hope God promises is not only a set of better circumstances. It is Himself. Even in exile, even in the long middle of a story, He can be found.

That may be the most hopeful part of all. You might be waiting on a diagnosis, a prodigal child, a marriage that needs healing, or a season of loneliness that has lasted longer than you expected. Jeremiah 29 doesn't promise that the wait will be short. It promises that God is not absent from it, and that the people who seek Him in the waiting find Him there.

If you are in your own season of waiting, try reading Jeremiah 29:1–14 in one sitting. Notice what God asks His people to do while they wait, and what He promises to be for them. Then build, plant, pray, and seek Him right where you are.

Questions women often ask

What is the context of Jeremiah 29:11?

It comes from a letter Jeremiah sent to Israelites exiled in Babylon (Jeremiah 29:1). God told them to settle in for seventy years, seek the welfare of the city, and trust that His plans for their future were good.

Is Jeremiah 29:11 a promise for Christians today?

It was first spoken to Israel in exile, so it isn't a guarantee of a specific personal outcome. But it reveals God's faithful character, and Scripture says all God's promises find their Yes in him, in Christ (2 Corinthians 1:20).

Does Jeremiah 29:11 mean God will make me prosper?

Not in the sense of wealth or an easy life. The Hebrew word is shalom, meaning wholeness and well-being under God's care, which the exiles experienced through a long season of hardship and waiting.
