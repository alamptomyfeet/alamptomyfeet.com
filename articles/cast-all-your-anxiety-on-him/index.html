Cast All Your Anxiety on Him

What 1 Peter 5:7 really means, and why Peter of all people could write it.

October 16, 2026 · 4 min read

“Humble yourselves, therefore, under the mighty hand of God so that at the proper time he may exalt you, casting all your anxieties on him, because he cares for you.”

1 Peter 5:6–7 (ESV)

Few verses are written on more index cards and bathroom mirrors than this one. Many of us know it as "cast all your anxiety on him," and in the ESV it reads casting all your anxieties on him, because he cares for you. Either way, it is one of the most tender sentences in the New Testament.

Here is what it means. 1 Peter 5:7 invites you to throw the full weight of every worry onto God, decisively and completely, because He personally cares for you. And it is part of a larger sentence that begins with humility. Handing your anxieties to God is what humbling yourself under His hand looks like in real life.

A letter to people under pressure

Peter wasn't writing to people with easy lives. He addresses his readers as elect exiles (1 Peter 1:1), believers scattered across what is now Turkey, living as outsiders and facing real hostility for their faith. He tells them, do not be surprised at the fiery trial when it comes upon you (1 Peter 4:12).

That context matters. This verse wasn't written for minor inconveniences, though God welcomes those too. It was written for people with genuine reasons to be afraid. Peter isn't telling them their worries are silly. He's telling them where to put them. He was most likely writing from Rome, which he seems to call Babylon (1 Peter 5:13), though scholars discuss the exact setting. Either way, he knew pressure firsthand.

What it means to cast

The word Peter uses for casting is the Greek epiripto, to throw something onto something else. It appears only one other time in the New Testament, on Palm Sunday, when the disciples brought the colt to Jesus, and throwing their cloaks on the colt, they set Jesus on it (Luke 19:35).

Picture that. Not a careful handing over, but a decisive throw, the whole cloak at once. That is the image Peter chooses for what we do with our worries. Not nudging them toward God while keeping a grip on the corner. Throwing them onto Him.

And notice the word all. Not the respectable worries, the ones that sound spiritual enough to pray about. All of them: the health scare, the strained marriage, the bank balance, the child who won't call back, the fear you've never said out loud.

Humility and the weight we carry

It's easy to read verse 7 on its own, but in the original it is grammatically tied to verse 6. Humble yourselves, therefore, under the mighty hand of God... casting all your anxieties on him. The casting is the humbling.

That is a surprising connection. We don't usually think of worry as a humility issue, and Peter isn't shaming anyone here. But often, underneath our anxiety, there is a quiet assumption that we have to hold everything together, that if we stop gripping, it will all fall apart. Humility is admitting that you were never meant to carry it all, and that God's hand is mighty enough to hold what yours cannot.

That isn't a loss. It's a relief. And Peter attaches a promise: at the proper time he may exalt you. God's timing, not ours, but a real promise nonetheless.

Because He cares for you

The reason Peter gives is simple and personal: because he cares for you. Not because you've earned it or finally prayed the right way. Because He cares.

There is something moving about Peter writing those words. Years earlier, during a storm on the Sea of Galilee, Jesus' disciples shook Him awake and cried, Teacher, do you not care that we are perishing? (Mark 4:38). Peter was very likely in that boat. In Mark's Greek, the word for care there is the same one Peter uses here. The man who once wondered whether Jesus cared now writes, with settled confidence, that He does.

Peter learned that over a lifetime: through the storm, through his own denial, through a breakfast by the sea when the risen Jesus restored him. The care he writes about isn't theory. It's the care that went to the cross for him, and for you.

Practicing it today

Casting isn't usually a one-time event. You may throw a worry onto God in the morning and find yourself picking it back up by lunch. That's all right. Throw it again. It can help to pair each worry with a truth from the passage: His hand is mighty, so this isn't too heavy for Him, and He cares for you, so it isn't too small for Him either.

Today, try naming your anxieties specifically, out loud or on paper, and giving each one to God by name. Then read 1 Peter 5:6–11 and notice how Peter ends: the God of all grace will himself restore, confirm, strengthen, and establish you. The One you are casting onto is strong enough, and He cares.

Questions women often ask

Is it 'anxiety' or 'anxieties' in 1 Peter 5:7?

Translations differ. The ESV says all your anxieties, while others say “all your anxiety.” Either way, the point is the same: Peter says all of it belongs in God's hands.

What if I cast my worries on God and then pick them back up?

That happens to nearly everyone. Casting is a practice more than a single moment. Each time you notice the weight again, you can give it back to Him.

Does casting my anxiety on God mean I stop planning or taking responsibility?

No. Scripture values wisdom and diligence. Casting your anxiety means you stop carrying the outcome as if it all depends on you, while still doing the faithful next thing in front of you.
